Every number on screen, and where it comes from

Plain-words notes for the prototype. Each value links to its source. A hollow circle means a proxy (real data from a similar place) or an assumption (no public data found), exactly as on the site.

The idea in 30 seconds

  • One extra light. Each traffic signal gets a blue light that switches on only when an ambulance is coming.
  • n+1. The junction the ambulance is approaching and the next one react together. The first blinks blue and goes green for the ambulance. The next turns green early with a steady blue light, so its queue has drained by the time the ambulance gets there.
  • When it switches. The ambulance sends its GPS position and speed every couple of seconds. The junction turns green when the ambulance is 94 s away S14, or as a backup when it is within 760 m S14, whichever comes first.
  • Why it matters. A siren reaches only about 12 m into a car at a junction S13. The blue light warns drivers hundreds of metres earlier, and the early green clears the queue the ambulance would otherwise be stuck behind.
  • No lane rule. We dropped the middle-lane idea. All lanes keep moving; the gain comes from the early greens.
Say it like thisDrivers hear a siren with seconds to react. Our blue light gives them a minute and a half, and turns the next two signals green so the queue is gone before the ambulance arrives.

Stuck behind cars waiting out Silk Board's 240 s red. About 587 s fully stopped. Average 3.2 km/h.

With the system

22 min 9 s

Silk Board turns green 760 m ahead, so the queue drains first. 0 s stopped. Average 4.6 km/h.

Benchmark

21 min 24 s

1,712 m at a steady 4.8 km/h. With the system the ambulance gets within a minute of it.

Say it like thisAlmost all of the 10 minutes is the wait at Silk Board's red light. The ambulance's road gets 45 seconds of green in a 285-second cycle. With the system the signal goes green 760 m ahead, so the queue has cleared before the ambulance gets there.

The ambulance never obeys a red light in either run. The siren-only delay comes from the cars in front of it that do.

Every calculation, step by step

Flood speed (the 24 km/h)

v(w) = 0.0009·w² − 0.5529·w + 86.9448 w = water depth (mm), v = safe speed (km/h) v(150) = 0.0009 × 22,500 − 0.5529 × 150 + 86.9448 = 20.25 − 82.94 + 86.94 = 24.26 km/h → stored as 24 At 300 mm or more the road counts as impassable (v = 0).

From Pregnolato et al. (2017) S9. Table: 0 mm → 86.9, 100 mm → 40.7, 150 mm → 24.3, 250 mm → 5.0, 300 mm → 0 km/h.

The 94 s trigger

29 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 8.06 m/s (clear-night Bengaluru speed, S1) 760 m ÷ 8.06 m/s = 94.3 s (the pilot's 760 m, S14) ETA = distance ÷ speed (checked on every GPS packet) Turn green when ETA ≤ 94.3 s OR distance ≤ 760 m

94.3 s is the warning the real pilot gives on a clear road. The 760 m backup covers an ambulance stuck in a jam: its speed is near zero, so its ETA never drops.

Unit conversions

km/h → m/s : divide by 3.6 4.8 km/h = 1.33 m/s 24 km/h = 6.67 m/s seconds → min:s 1,953 s = 32 min 33 s 1,329 s = 22 min 9 s Screen time = real time ÷ 15 22 min 9 s real ≈ 89 s on screen

Journey distance and the benchmark

1,000 m before J1 + 405 m J1→J2 + 7 m (half of J2's box) + 300 m after = 1,712 m 1,712 m ÷ 1.33 m/s = 1,284 s ≈ 21 min 24 s (steady 4.8 km/h, no stops)

The 405 m between the junctions is measured on the OpenStreetMap stand-in streets S20.

Time saved

Saved = journey without − journey with = 1,953.0 − 1,329.4 = 623.6 s = 10 min 24 s

A morning count, used for the evening as a proxy. The greens add to 330 s in a 285 s cycle, so BTM and HSR must overlap; we overlap them by 60 s.

How many lanes

Junction capacity (IRC): 525 × road width (m) passenger-car units per hour of green (S11) Hosur S: 2,379 veh/h × 0.606 PCU/veh × 285 s ÷ 45 s = 9,137 PCU per hour of green 9,137 ÷ 525 = 17.4 m → 5 lanes of 3.5 m

0.606 is the average size of a vehicle in car units, from the vehicle mix S7 and car-unit values S10. So “5 lanes” means the least road that can carry the measured traffic in the measured green time. Read it as effective lanes: Indian traffic is not lane-disciplined.

Signal changes

Amber 3 s + all-red 2 s = 5 s between greens (picked from IRC ranges 3–6 s and 1–2 s, S12)

Every switch to green, including the system's, goes through this 5 s change, so no road ever jumps straight from green to red.

How the simulation works, in plain words

  • Real scale. Every speed, distance and timing is real. Only the playback clock is sped up 15×.
  • Car-following. Each vehicle speeds up, follows and brakes using the Intelligent Driver Model, a standard traffic model S16. Every vehicle gets its own desired speed around the scenario speed; two-wheelers ride a little faster and buses a little slower.
  • Groups of 10. To keep it fast, the engine moves traffic in groups of 10 vehicles of one type (assumption). On screen each group is drawn as its 10 vehicles.
  • Traffic in. Vehicles arrive on each road at the hourly counts (S5 for Silk Board), divided by 10.
  • Junction capacity. A green light lets traffic through no faster than the IRC capacity formula allows S11, so queues drain at a realistic rate.
  • Warm-up. Before the ambulance appears the simulation runs three full signal cycles, so the queues are already there.
  • Fair comparison. Both runs use the same random seed: identical traffic until the ambulance arrives.
  • The ambulance. It ignores red lights in both runs and never overtakes. It is slowed only by the vehicles ahead in its lane.
  • Deterministic. The same scenario always gives the same result, so scrubbing backwards shows exactly what happened.

Likely questions

Is 10 minutes realistic?

It is a simulation estimate. Nearly all of it is the wait at Silk Board's red: the ambulance's road gets 45 s of green in a 285 s cycle S5, so a siren-only ambulance sits in the queue for about 10 minutes. The saving is also conservative, because our ambulance never overtakes.

Why does the ambulance only average 4.6 km/h when its limit is 24?

Because it never overtakes. It moves at the speed of the traffic in front of it, which is 4.8 km/h in the rain S2. The 24 km/h is the flood-safe ceiling S9, not a speed it reaches.

Why does the night scenario save nothing?

There is almost no traffic, so there is no queue to clear. The system only helps where there is a queue. That is the control case, and it shows we aren't inflating results.

Where does 760 m come from?

A real Bengaluru pilot turned signals green when the ambulance was 760 m away S14. We use it as the backup trigger distance, and derive the 94 s rule from it.

Why trigger on time and not just distance?

A fixed distance holds cross traffic at red for very different times: about a minute and a half for a fast ambulance, many minutes for one crawling in a jam. A time rule gives drivers the same warning every time. The distance backup is there because a stuck ambulance's ETA never drops.

Doesn't holding everyone else at red cause new jams?

Every other road is held only while the ambulance approaches and passes. The junction then goes through amber and all-red and carries on its cycle exactly where it stopped, so no road loses its turn. We haven't measured the knock-on delay to cross traffic; that would be a good next step.

Why only 15× speed-up?

So a 285 s Silk Board cycle plays in about 19 s: fast enough to watch, slow enough to follow. Only the clock changes; relative speeds stay true, so a 4.8 km/h scene looks exactly 6× slower than a 29 km/h one.

Why is the Bangalore map not Silk Board?

Our OpenStreetMap export covers Indiranagar, not Silk Board. The 3D streets are a stand-in; the names, traffic counts and signal timings are Silk Board's. Re-exporting the real area is listed as the next step. The same applies to Gurugram.

Why are cars in groups of 10?

Silk Board moves 17,816 vehicles an hour. Simulating each one would be slow in a browser, so the engine moves groups of 10 and each group follows the same driving rules, with spacing set so steady flow still matches the IRC capacity S11. On screen you see the individual vehicles.

What to admit before you're asked

  1. Stand-in maps. The 3D streets in both cities are an OpenStreetMap stand-in, not the real junctions. The J1→J2 distances are measured on those streets.
  2. Unverified second junction. “Next signal towards Madiwala” is unconfirmed, and its 150 s timing is an assumption.
  3. One rain observation. 4.8 km/h is from one evening on the Outer Ring Road S2; Silk Board's own 4.48 km/h S6 supports it.
  4. Morning count for an evening scene. Silk Board's volumes are a 9:30–10:30 am count S5.
  5. Assumed water depth. 150 mm (Bangalore) and 250 mm (Gurugram) are assumptions.
  6. No overtaking. Our ambulance never passes traffic, so the saving is conservative.
  7. Estimates. All journey times and savings come from the simulation, not a field trial.
