Emergency Enroute · jury notes

How the system works, and how we tested it

These notes go from the idea, to the prototype we built to show it, to the simulation underneath and the data it runs on. Every number has a source tag like S2, which links to the source list at the end.

A hollow circle next to a number means it is a proxy (real data from a similar place) or an assumption (no public data was found). The site marks them the same way.

Part 1

The concept

What the system is, why it is needed, and how it would work on a real street.

The problem: a siren warns too late

Today an ambulance has only its siren. By the time a driver hears it, there is almost no time left to react, and the cars around them are stuck in the same queue at a red light.

  • At a city junction a siren carries only about 12 m into a closed car S13.
  • With windows closed, drivers work out where the siren is coming from only 26% of the time S13. Reacting takes about 1.6 s S13.
  • 62% of ambulance drivers say motorists don't give way S17. Response times in Indian cities run to 25–30 min S17.
  • Bengaluru has about 3,000 ambulance trips a day, and deaths on the way to hospital rose from 175 in 2021 to 292 in 2023 S15.

Even drivers who want to move often can't. When the light ahead is red, the queue has nowhere to go.

The idea: one extra blue light, two junctions ahead

Every traffic signal gets one extra light, a blue one. It switches on only when an ambulance is approaching.

The system works on n + 1: the junction the ambulance is heading for and the one after it react at the same moment.

  • Both junctions turn green for the ambulance's road well before it arrives. The queue in front of it drains while it is still hundreds of metres away.
  • Drivers on the other roads see the blue light and know why they are being held at red.
  • Nobody has to change lanes or pull over. There is no middle-lane rule; every lane keeps moving.
Say it like thisDrivers hear a siren with seconds to react. Our blue light warns them more than a minute and a half ahead, and turns the next two signals green so the queue is gone before the ambulance gets there.

What each junction does

Each junction is always in one of four states. The blue light tells drivers which one: blinking means the ambulance is about to cross here, steady means it is on its way.

MomentFirst junction (J1)Next junction (J2)
No ambulanceNormal: usual signal cycle, blue light offNormal
Ambulance comes within range of J1Alert: blue light blinking. Green for the ambulance's road, red for all the others.Prewarn: blue light steady. Green for the ambulance's road, red for the others, so this queue clears early too.
Ambulance passes J1Cleared: goes back to its normal cycleAlert: blue light starts blinking. The green stays on.
Ambulance passes J2NormalCleared, then Normal

Every change to green, and the return to the normal cycle, goes through amber (3 s) and all-red (2 s) first S12, so no road jumps straight from green to red. After the ambulance passes, the junction carries on its cycle from where it was interrupted.

When it switches on

The ambulance sends its position and speed every couple of seconds. Each time, the junction works out how long the ambulance will take to arrive:

time to arrive (ETA) = distance to the junction ÷ ambulance speed

The junction switches to Alert when either of these is true, whichever comes first:

  1. The ambulance is 94.3 s or less away S14. This is the main rule.
  2. The ambulance is within 760 m S14. This is the backup for an ambulance stuck in a jam: its speed is close to zero, so its ETA never drops.

Both numbers come from a real pilot in Bengaluru, where a device turned signals green when an ambulance was 760 m away S14. The 94.3 s is how long 760 m takes on a clear road (worked out in the calculations).

The hardware, in three layers

1 · In the ambulance

A GPS unit (NEO-M8N) and a motion sensor (MPU9250) send {lat, lon, heading, speed, timestamp} every 1–2 s over 4G or LoRa.

2 · Central routing

Matches the position to the city map, finds the next signals on the route, and works out the distance (haversine formula), the direction check and the ETA to each.

3 · At the junction

A controller (Raspberry Pi or ESP32) switches the blue light with a relay and overrides the signal phase. Junctions pass the message on to the next one over MQTT. An ANPR camera confirms the ambulance has passed.

An optional backup listens for the siren with a microphone and a sound classifier, in case GPS or the network drops. None of this hardware has been built or field-tested; the prototype shows what it would do.

Part 2

How we show it: the prototype

A web page that plays the system out on real streets, so the jury can watch it work instead of reading about it.

What's on screen

The screen has two parts. On the left is a text panel with the scenario, the result and the state of each signal. On the right is a dark 3D city built from real OpenStreetMap buildings and roads S20, with the ambulance's route running through it.

There are two pages, both using the same 3D city:

  • Map: a tilted view of the whole route and both junctions.
  • Junction: the camera moves down to one junction to show its arms, lanes, signal heads and queues.

Two cities can be picked at the bottom right: Gurgaon (Monday evening peak) and Bangalore (heavy evening rain).

Part 3

How the simulation works

What is computed underneath the picture, and where each input comes from.

In short

For each scenario we build a stretch of road with two signalled junctions, fill it with traffic at the real volumes and speeds, and run the real signal timings. Then we send an ambulance through it twice: once with only a siren, once with the blue-light system. Everything else is identical. The difference in journey time is the headline result.

The picture only reads the simulation. Nothing on screen moves on its own; every vehicle, light and number is the simulation's state at that moment.

The road

  • The ambulance starts 1,000 m before J1 , far enough that it always enters before any trigger can fire.
  • J1 to J2 is 405 m in Bangalore and 514 m in Gurgaon S20, measured on the OpenStreetMap streets we drew.
  • The run ends 300 m after J2 .
  • Each junction has four arms. Cross roads are drawn 300 m long. Lanes are 3.5 m wide .

The simulation runs along a straight line. That line is then laid onto the real OpenStreetMap road, so the distances on the map and in the simulation are the same.

The traffic

  • How many. Vehicles arrive on each road at its hourly volume. At Silk Board these are the measured counts (17,816 vehicles an hour in total, 8,300 from BTM Layout) S5. Where no count exists, the volume is set from the road's capacity S11 and an assumed load: 95% of capacity at peak, 105% for Gurgaon at 6 pm, 25% at night .
  • What kind. Two-wheelers, cars, autos and buses in a set mix. Bengaluru: 55% / 25% / 12% / 8%. Gurgaon: 40% / 42% / 10% / 8% . The Bengaluru mix sits between the registered fleet (68% two-wheelers) S7 and roadside counts (39–44%) S10.
  • How they drive. Each vehicle speeds up, follows the one in front and brakes using the Intelligent Driver Model, a standard traffic model S16. Each gets its own preferred speed, spread around the scenario's traffic speed (two-wheelers 10% faster, buses 15% slower ), so they don't all move in step.
  • In groups of 10. To run in a browser, the simulation moves traffic in groups of 10 vehicles of one type . A group's spacing is set so it flows like 10 vehicles would. On screen you see the individual vehicles.
  • Turning. 60% of the traffic on the ambulance's road at J1 carries on to J2; the rest turns off .

The signals

  • Silk Board uses its measured timing: a 285 s cycle with greens of 150 s for BTM Layout, 90 s for HSR Layout and 45 s for each side of Hosur Road S5. These add up to more than 285 s, so BTM and HSR must overlap. We overlap them by 60 s .
  • The ambulance comes in on Hosur Road, so on a normal cycle it gets 45 s of green and 240 s of red.
  • Junctions with no published timing (both Gurgaon junctions and the second Bangalore junction) use a 150 s, four-phase cycle : 58 s for the ambulance's road, then 34, 24 and 14 s for the others.
  • Between greens: amber 3 s and all-red 2 s, picked from the Indian Roads Congress ranges S12.
  • How fast a queue drains. A green light lets traffic through no faster than the IRC capacity formula allows: 525 × road width in metres, in car units per hour of green S11. Buses count as 2.2 cars, autos 0.78, two-wheelers 0.46 S10.
  • Both junctions' cycles start together; they are not coordinated , which is typical in Indian cities.

The ambulance

  • It drives in the middle lane and never overtakes. It is held back only by the vehicles in front of it.
  • It ignores red lights in both runs. The siren-only delay therefore comes from the queue of cars in front of it, not from the ambulance waiting at an empty red light.
  • Its preferred speed depends on the run. Siren only: no faster than traffic , because drivers ahead can't hear it in time S13 and most don't give way S17. With the system: a higher target (24 km/h in the Bangalore rain, the flood-safe speed S9; 35 km/h in Gurgaon). It only reaches it where the road ahead is clear.
  • It sends a GPS packet every 1.5 s (the concept says every 1–2 s). The junctions run the trigger rule on each packet, so a trigger fires within one packet of the threshold. That is why the logs show 759 m rather than exactly 760 m.
  • It has “passed” a junction when its rear clears the far side of the junction box.

A fair comparison

  • Same traffic. Both runs use the same random seed, so the traffic is identical until the system first changes a light.
  • Queues already there. The simulation runs three full signal cycles before the ambulance appears , so it meets real queues, not an empty road.
  • Real speeds. Only the playback clock is sped up (15×). A 4.8 km/h scene looks exactly 6× slower than a 29 km/h one.
  • Repeatable. The same scenario always gives the same result, and scrubbing backwards shows exactly what happened.
  • Rain done properly. Rain itself barely slows Indian traffic; standing water does S21. So the rain scenarios slow traffic by water depth, using a published flood-speed formula S9, not a general “rain is slower” factor.

The calculations, step by step

The 94.3 s trigger

Clear-night Bengaluru speed: 29 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 8.06 m/s (S1) Pilot's trigger distance: 760 m (S14) Time to cover it: 760 m ÷ 8.06 m/s = 94.3 s Rule: switch on when ETA ≤ 94.3 s OR distance ≤ 760 m

The 29 km/h itself: TomTom gives 36 min 9 s per 10 km at 74.4% congestion S1, so free flow is about 36.15 ÷ 1.744 ≈ 20.7 min per 10 km, or 29 km/h.

Flood speed (the 24 km/h)

v(w) = 0.0009·w² − 0.5529·w + 86.9448 w = water depth (mm), v = safe speed (km/h) v(150) = 0.0009 × 22,500 − 0.5529 × 150 + 86.9448 = 20.25 − 82.94 + 86.94 = 24.26 km/h → used as 24 v(250) ≈ 5.0 km/h (the Gurgaon waterlogging case) 300 mm or more: impassable

From Pregnolato et al. (2017) S9. The water depths, 150 mm and 250 mm, are our assumptions.

Part 5

For the jury

What to say up front, and answers to the questions most likely to come up.

What to admit before you're asked

  1. Stand-in maps. Our OpenStreetMap exports cover Indiranagar (Bangalore) and Sector 39 (Gurgaon), not Silk Board or Rajiv Chowk. The names, traffic counts and timings are the real junctions'; the streets drawn are stand-ins, and the J1→J2 distances are measured on them.
  2. Unconfirmed junctions. “Next signal towards Madiwala” is not confirmed. Rajiv Chowk may no longer be a signalled junction at street level. Timings at both Gurgaon junctions and at Bangalore's J2 are assumptions.
  3. One rain observation. 4.8 km/h comes from one evening on the Outer Ring Road S2. Silk Board's own 4.48 km/h S6 supports it.
  4. Morning count, evening scene. Silk Board's volumes are a 9:30–10:30 am count S5.
  5. Part of the saving is the speed assumption. In Bangalore, 1 min 57 s of the 10 min 24 s comes before any light changes, from the ambulance's higher target speed with the system. The signals account for the rest.
  6. Assumed water depths. 150 mm and 250 mm are our assumptions.
  7. No overtaking. Our ambulance never passes other vehicles. Real ambulances do, so real times would be shorter in both runs.
  8. Cross traffic not measured. We haven't measured the extra delay to drivers held at red on the other roads.
  9. Estimates. Every journey time and saving comes from the simulation, not a field trial.

Likely questions

Is 10 minutes realistic?

It is a simulation estimate. Just over 9 minutes of it is saved on the approach to Silk Board, where the ambulance's road gets only 45 s of green in a 285 s cycle S5. About 2 minutes comes from our assumption that the ambulance drives faster with the system. Because our ambulance never overtakes, the times in both runs are on the slow side.

Isn't some of the saving just the faster ambulance?

In Bangalore, 1 min 57 s is, yes: that part happens before any light changes. We say so openly. The remaining 8 min 27 s comes from the signals. In Gurgaon the speed share is 8.7 s of 72.8 s. In the waterlogging case the water caps both runs at the same speed, so all of the 2 min 19 s comes from the signals.

Why does the ambulance only average 4.6 km/h when its target is 24?

Because it never overtakes. It moves at the speed of the traffic in front of it, which is 4.8 km/h in the rain S2. 24 km/h is the flood-safe ceiling S9, reached only where the road ahead is clear.

Why does the night scenario save nothing?

There is almost no traffic, so there is no queue to clear, and our ambulance ignores red lights in both runs. The system helps only where there is a queue. It is the control case, and it shows we are not inflating results.

Where does 760 m come from?

A real Bengaluru pilot turned signals green when an ambulance was 760 m away S14. We use it as the backup distance and work out the 94.3 s rule from it.

Why trigger on time and not just distance?

A fixed distance holds cross traffic for very different lengths of time: about a minute and a half for a fast ambulance, many minutes for one crawling in a jam. A time rule gives drivers the same warning every time. The distance backup is there because a stuck ambulance's ETA never drops.

Why warn the next junction too?

Because a queue takes time to drain. If J2 waited until the ambulance passed J1, the ambulance would reach J2's queue before it cleared. In Gurgaon, 58.8 s of the 72.8 s saved is at the second junction.

Doesn't holding everyone else at red cause new jams?

Other roads are held only while the ambulance approaches and passes. The junction then goes through amber and all-red and carries on its cycle from where it stopped, so no road loses its turn. We haven't measured the knock-on delay to cross traffic; that would be a good next step.

Why speed playback up only 15×?

So a 285 s Silk Board cycle plays in about 19 s: fast enough to watch, slow enough to follow. Only the clock changes, so relative speeds stay true.

Why are the maps not the real junctions?

Our OpenStreetMap exports cover other parts of each city. The streets are stand-ins; the junction names, traffic counts and signal timings are the real ones. Exporting the real corridors is the next step.

Why move cars in groups of 10?

Silk Board moves 17,816 vehicles an hour. Simulating each one would be too slow in a browser. Groups of 10 follow the same driving rules, with spacing set so they flow like 10 vehicles would, and queues drain at the IRC capacity S11. On screen you see the individual vehicles.
